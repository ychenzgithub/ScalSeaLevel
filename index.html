Southern California coast · Winter 2026–27

A near-record El Niño is building. How high will the sea stand on the Southern California coast?

When a strong El Niño takes hold, the ocean along Southern California runs several inches higher than normal for months. That extra height rides on top of every tide, so winter king tides and storm waves reach farther up beaches, harbors and low-lying streets. This page brings together every tide-gauge record we could find for the region, back to 1906, and sets them beside NOAA's October 2026 El Niño forecast.

History

What 120 years of tide gauges show

Tide gauges in Southern California have recorded the height of the sea every hour since 1906 in San Diego and since the 1920s in La Jolla and Los Angeles. In El Niño winters the readings run high month after month. Unlike rainfall, which El Niño only nudges, sea level follows it closely: the stronger the El Niño, the higher the winter sea, with few exceptions.

This El Niño

2026 against the giants

In the very strong El Niños of 1982–83 and 1997–98, Southern California's sea level began climbing in late summer or fall and stayed high through spring. The question for this winter is whether 2026 follows the same path.

Timing and extremes

When the water runs highest, and why

El Niño lifts Southern California's sea level from late summer into spring, with the peak usually between November and January. The very highest water levels come when a raised sea lines up with the year's biggest tides, in winter or early summer, sometimes with a storm surge on top.

Along the coast

El Niño's high water travels up the coast

Southern California sits in the middle of a coastline that feels El Niño from Chile to Alaska. Tide gauges in Peru and Ecuador rise first, often by late spring, and the high water spreads north along Mexico and California over the following months as one ocean wave after another travels up the coast. Farther north, in Oregon, Washington and Alaska, El Niño's winter storms push the sea even higher than in Southern California.

Caveats

What these numbers do and don't tell you

El Niño raises the stage; tides and storms decide the flooding

A winter sea a few inches above normal causes no harm by itself. The trouble comes when it coincides with the year's highest tides, a storm surge and large waves. The seasonal numbers here shift the odds of those moments; they cannot say which day they will happen.

Today's baseline is higher

Sea level along the coast has risen since 1983 and 1998, so the same El Niño boost now lands on a higher starting point. The “at 2026–27 sea level” figures on this page add that rise back in; tide tables do not.

This event may go beyond the record

NOAA gives 70–83% odds of an El Niño stronger than any since 1950. No statistical method can know how the sea responds beyond what has been observed. The “If El Niño is stronger than any on record” switch shows how much the answer depends on that assumption.

Waves are not in these numbers

Tide gauges sit in harbors and on piers, where they measure the still-water level and filter out waves. On open beaches, waves running up the shore can add several feet more. Much of the damage in the 1982–83 and 2015–16 El Niños came from waves and erosion on top of high water [7, 8, 9].

Not every El Niño lifts the sea the same way

The sea level response depends on how much of El Niño's warm water reaches the coast through the ocean, and how stormy the winter is. Spring sea levels, the North Pacific's state and the local winds all add their own variation.

A gauge measures the sea relative to the land

Tide gauges record relative sea level, so sinking or rising land shows up as sea level change. Terminal Island in Long Beach sank several meters as oil was pumped from beneath it in the mid-1900s. This analysis removes each gauge's long-term trend, which takes out slow land motion but keeps the year-to-year swings that El Niño causes.

Be ready

What a high-water El Niño winter can bring

Most high-water days are a nuisance: a flooded parking lot or a closed coastal road at the top of the tide. A few, when a storm arrives on a king tide, cause real damage.

High tide flooding

Low-lying harbor and beach neighborhoods can see salt water in streets and storm drains near the peak of king tides, even on sunny days. Check the tide tables for the dates in the King tides section, and watch for NWS coastal flood advisories.

Beach erosion and cliff collapse

Higher water lets waves reach farther up the beach and strip away sand. In the 2015–16 El Niño winter, beach erosion along the West Coast was far above normal, and at many beaches it was the largest ever measured [8]. In Southern California, cliffs eroded where beaches thinned [9].

Harbors, piers and coastal roads

The strongest El Niño winters, 1982–83 and 1997–98, brought some of the most damaging coastal erosion on record around the Pacific [7]. Boats, docks, piers and seawalls take the brunt when surge and waves arrive at high tide.

Where to get forecasts and warnings

NOAA Tides & Currents (live water levels and tide predictions); NWS Los Angeles/Oxnard and San Diego (coastal flood advisories); CDIP wave forecasts (Scripps); NOAA's high tide flooding outlook; the California King Tides Project; and USGS CoSMoS coastal flood maps on Our Coast, Our Future.

Methods

Data and methods

Tide gauges
Monthly mean sea level, hourly water levels and hourly tide predictions from NOAA CO-OPS for every Southern California station with a usable record [17]: San Diego (1906–), La Jolla (1924–), Los Angeles (1923–), Santa Monica (1933–), Santa Barbara (1973–), and the retired Newport Beach, Long Beach (Terminal Island), Rincon Island and Platform Harvest gauges. Ensenada, Mexico (1956–1990) comes from the Permanent Service for Mean Sea Level [18]. Gauges along the rest of the Pacific coast come from NOAA CO-OPS (United States) and the University of Hawaii Sea Level Center (other countries) [19]. October 2026 is estimated from NOAA's preliminary 6‑minute data through October 25: the month's average predicted tide plus the average difference between observed and predicted water so far.
Normal and anomalies
For each gauge we remove the average seasonal cycle and a slowly rising baseline: a straight-line fit through the surrounding 19 years, the length of a National Tidal Datum Epoch. Near the start and end of a record, the 19-year window is shifted rather than shortened, so the baseline for 2026–27 is the trend of the last 19 years. Before fitting the baseline, the part of each month explained by El Niño is removed, so that big events do not pull the baseline up. What remains is the sea level anomaly. The regional average is the mean anomaly of the five long-running gauges reporting that month.
Highest water
The highest hourly water level of each November–March, above Mean Higher High Water (MHHW, 1983–2001 epoch), adjusted to 2026–27 sea level by adding the rise in the baseline between that winter and January 2027 (for retired gauges, the rise after the gauge closed is taken from the nearest running gauge). Flood thresholds are NOAA's derived minor, moderate and major high tide flooding levels [20]. The components in the top-10 table split each event into the predicted tide, that month's average difference between observed and predicted water, and the remainder (storm surge over hours to days).
El Niño index and forecast
NOAA's Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI), official since February 2026 [1, 4], averaged over November–January as each winter's El Niño strength. Before 1950 we rebuilt the same index from ERSSTv5 [5]; it matches the official index with r = 0.992. The forecast is NOAA CPC's October 2026 RONI outlook [2]: November–January median +2.65 °C, 5–95% range +2.00 to +3.31 °C, approximated as a normal distribution with a spread of 0.40 °C.
Statistical model
For each gauge and measure, a linear regression with separate El Niño and La Niña slopes: mean = b0 + bLN·min(x, 0) + bEN·max(x, 0), where x is the November–January RONI, with normally distributed scatter that is allowed to be larger in El Niño winters (x ≥ 0.5). Fitted by maximum likelihood. Each projection combines the El Niño forecast, 150 bootstrap refits and the scatter, in 20,000 Monte Carlo draws that your browser runs each time a setting changes.
Validation
Leave-one-out cross-validation compares the model with a climatology that ignores El Niño, using the continuous ranked probability score [21].
Satellite maps
AVISO monthly absolute dynamic topography (1993–2009) and NOAA CoastWatch's blended near-real-time sea level anomaly (2015 onward), both via NOAA ERDDAP and subsampled to 1° [22, 23]. Anomalies are relative to the 1993–2009 AVISO average for each month.
Limitations
Few very strong El Niños exist to learn from, and this one may exceed them all. Monthly and seasonal averages say nothing about the timing of individual storms. Gauges measure relative sea level in harbors and do not capture waves. The model ignores the El Niño's spatial pattern, the state of the North Pacific and how the season starts. It is an independent statistical analysis, not an official forecast.
